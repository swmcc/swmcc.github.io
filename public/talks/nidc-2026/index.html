Now, back to that third corner, ship it, then watch, because I have a confession, and it's true in both worlds.

We both still make that work wait before it ships. QA sign-off at Whitespace, and me at home. We treat it like a one-way door, but it isn't one. We can undo it.

And there are good reasons we got here. For most of our careers, releases were expensive and rollbacks were slow and scary. If you couldn't take something back quickly, the only safe place to catch a problem was before it went out. So we built our habits, our process and our sign-offs around that.

But for this kind of change, that's no longer true. With a feature flag, a rollback that takes one command, and someone who actually looks at it once it's live, you can catch the problem after release, faster, and with real users telling you what's wrong instead of a test plan guessing.

None of that is new, by the way. Continuous delivery people have been saying it for fifteen years. What's new is two things.

First, the volume. When a team shipped a handful of changes a week, holding each one for QA cost a day here and there. Now agents produce dozens of changes a day, and every one of them joins the same queue in front of the same few people. That queue is the grey.

The gates tell you where the human stands. The controls are how you actually run each gate.

A control is anything that reduces risk. It's the word auditors and regulated industries use, and it comes in three kinds.

Some prevent: they stop the problem happening. A lock on the door.

Some detect: they tell you it's happened. The smoke alarm.

Some correct: they limit the damage afterwards. The backup you restore from.

Each of the next six slides tells you which kind it is, and which gate it runs on. They're all borrowed from the regulated world.

Govern the undo, not the headcount.

Most of us set our governance by headcount. A bigger team gets more process. More people, more sign-offs, more meetings. A small team gets almost none.

The size of the team tells you nothing about the risk of the change. How hard it is to undo tells you nearly everything.
